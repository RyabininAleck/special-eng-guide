## Pedagogical Foundation

This course is built on four research-backed pillars used by Cambridge Language Centre, Oxford's intensive English programs, and the Defense Language Institute:

1. **Explicit + Implicit learning** — rules are explained first (deductive), then discovered in reading (inductive). Research by Ellis (2005) shows adult learners benefit most from this combination.
2. **Comprehensible Input** (Krashen) — daily reading at i+1 level (just above current). The book provides this passively.
3. **Translation as a learning tool** — Laufer & Girsai (2008) research at University of Haifa proved translation exercises outperform free practice for adult grammar acquisition by 2.5x.
4. **Productive output + immediate conversation** — Swain's Output Hypothesis: you don't fully acquire grammar until you *produce* it under pressure. Your daily live lesson is exactly this.

**Daily architecture (proven by intensive programs at Middlebury Language Schools and DLI):**
```
Morning, hour 1 → Grammar theory: rules, examples, analysis (60 min)
Morning, hour 2 → Translation drills + written practice (60 min)
Afternoon       → Reading chapter + Comprehension questions (45 min)
Evening         → Live speaking lesson with teacher (45 min)
```

## Day 1 — Present Tenses: The Engine of English

### Grammar Block (60 min)

**The Core Principle:**
English present tenses are about *relationship to now*, not just "what happens now."
There are three present forms, each answering a different question about time:

---

#### 1. Present Simple — *permanent truth, habits, facts*

**Rule:** Subject + base verb (+ -s for he/she/it)

**When to use:**
- Facts that don't change: *Water boils at 100°C.*
- Habits and routines: *I write code every morning.*
- Scheduled future (timetables): *The flight departs at 9 AM.*
- Academic/scientific statements: *The algorithm runs in O(n log n) time.*

**Signal words:** always, usually, often, sometimes, never, every day/week/year

**Examples (tech/academic context):**
1. The CPU *executes* instructions in sequence.
2. He *studies* algorithms three hours a day.
3. Zhejiang University *accepts* international students each September.
4. This function *returns* a boolean value.
5. I *never skip* the fundamentals when learning something new.

**Common mistakes (Russian speakers):**
- ❌ *He go to university* → ✅ *He goes to university* (add -s for he/she/it)
- ❌ *I am know this* → ✅ *I know this* (state verbs don't use -ing: know, understand, believe, want, need, seem, contain)
- ❌ *Does he studies?* → ✅ *Does he study?* (question form drops -s)

**State verbs (never used in Continuous):**
know, understand, believe, think (opinion), want, need, prefer, like, love, hate, own, belong, contain, seem, appear, mean, depend

---

#### 2. Present Continuous — *action in progress or temporary situation*

**Rule:** Subject + am/is/are + verb-ing

**When to use:**
- Action happening right now: *I am reading a grammar rule.*
- Temporary situation (not permanent): *He is living in Shanghai for the semester.*
- Changing trends: *AI is becoming the dominant technology.*
- Future plans (arranged): *I am flying to Hangzhou next month.*
- Annoying repeated behavior (with "always"): *He is always interrupting during lectures.* (negative emotion implied)

**Examples:**
1. The model *is training* on 10 billion parameters right now.
2. She *is applying* to three universities this season.
3. Standards in this field *are evolving* rapidly.
4. I *am meeting* the admissions officer on Thursday.

---

#### 3. Present Perfect — *past action with present relevance*

**Rule:** Subject + have/has + past participle

**When to use:**
- Experience (at any point in life): *I have visited Shanghai.*
- Recent action with current result: *He has just fixed the bug.* (it's fixed now)
- Unfinished time period: *I have written three papers this year.* (year not over)
- Change over time: *Technology has transformed education.*

**Signal words:** just, already, yet, ever, never, recently, lately, so far, this week/month/year

**Examples:**
1. I *have worked* on embedded systems for two years.
2. She *has already submitted* her application.
3. *Have* you *ever* programmed in Rust?
4. The team *has not finished* the migration yet.
5. Researchers *have discovered* a new compression algorithm.

**Present Perfect vs. Past Simple (the key distinction):**
- *I visited China* — specific past time (even if unstated), not connected to now.
- *I have visited China* — life experience, relevant to who you are today.
- *Einstein discovered relativity in 1905* ← past (he's dead; fixed historical fact) ✅
- *Scientists have discovered a new exoplanet* ← present relevance (news) ✅

## Day 2 — Past Tenses: How We Tell Stories

### Grammar Block (60 min)

**The Core Principle:**
Past tenses in English aren't just about "what happened." They encode the *relationship between events* — which came first, what was background, what interrupted what.

---

#### 1. Past Simple — *completed action at a specific past time*

**Rule:** Subject + past form (regular: +ed, irregular: memorize)

**When to use:**
- Single completed action: *I wrote the script yesterday.*
- Series of events: *He opened the terminal, ran the command, and rebooted.*
- With specific time: *In 2019, I joined the bank as a developer.*

**Examples:**
1. I *started* learning English seriously two years ago.
2. The team *built* the telemetry system in six weeks.
3. Turing *proposed* the Turing Test in 1950.
4. She *didn't submit* her application on time.
5. *Did* you *read* the Feynman Lectures?

---

#### 2. Past Continuous — *background action interrupted or ongoing at a past moment*

**Rule:** Subject + was/were + verb-ing

**When to use:**
- Action in progress at a specific past time: *At 3 AM, I was debugging a memory leak.*
- Background to a main event: *I was reading the paper when the idea hit me.*
- Two parallel past actions: *While he was designing the circuit, she was writing the firmware.*

**Examples:**
1. When the server crashed, we *were deploying* the new build.
2. I *was studying* for the entrance exam when I got the acceptance letter.
3. The motor *was overheating* while the car *was running* the final lap.

**Past Simple vs. Past Continuous:**
- *I read the article.* — complete, done.
- *I was reading the article.* — in progress; implies something else happened, or it's the background.
- *He called while I was cooking.* — "calling" is the event; "cooking" is the background.

---

#### 3. Past Perfect — *action completed before another past action*

**Rule:** Subject + had + past participle

**When to use:**
- Action that happened before another past action: *By the time I arrived, they had already started.*
- Cause-and-effect in the past: *The build failed because someone had pushed broken code.*
- Reported speech (backshift): *She said she had finished the analysis.*

**Examples:**
1. Before I applied to Zhejiang, I *had already visited* three Chinese universities.
2. He *hadn't backed up* the data before the drive failed.
3. By 2023, I *had worked* on three different engineering teams.
4. I understood the concept because I *had studied* the math beforehand.

---

#### 4. Used to / Would — *past habits and states that no longer exist*

**Used to + infinitive:** past habit OR past state
- *I used to write everything in C++.* (habit, don't anymore)
- *This area used to be an industrial zone.* (state, changed)

**Would + infinitive:** only for past habits, NOT states
- *Every morning, we would review the logs and discuss anomalies.*
- ❌ *I would live in Moscow.* (state → must use "used to")

## Day 3 — Future Forms: Plans, Predictions, Decisions

### Grammar Block (60 min)

**The Core Principle:**
English has no "true future tense" — it uses modal verbs and present forms to express future meaning. Each form communicates a *different type of future* — the speaker's certainty, source of the plan, and degree of control.

---

#### 1. will + infinitive — *decisions made at the moment, predictions, facts*

**Use:**
- Spontaneous decision: *"The server is down." — "I'll restart it."*
- Prediction based on belief/opinion: *I think AI will replace routine coding jobs.*
- Future fact: *The exam will be in March.*
- Offers and promises: *I'll send you the file.*
- Threats and warnings: *The program will crash if you don't handle exceptions.*

---

#### 2. going to + infinitive — *planned intentions, predictions from evidence*

**Use:**
- Plan made before speaking: *I'm going to apply to three universities this fall.*
- Prediction from visible evidence: *Look at that memory leak — the process is going to crash.*

**Key distinction:**
- *I'll buy a new laptop.* — decided right now, probably spontaneous.
- *I'm going to buy a new laptop.* — I've already thought about this; it's a plan.

---

#### 3. Present Continuous — *fixed, arranged future (with another person or system)*

**Use:** For appointments, bookings, meetings — actions involving external arrangement.

- *I'm meeting the professor on Thursday.* (arranged)
- *The flight is leaving at 6 AM.* (booked)
- *We're deploying the new version on Friday.* (scheduled with team)

---

#### 4. Present Simple — *timetables, official schedules*

- *The exam starts at 9 AM.*
- *The semester begins in September.*

---

#### 5. Future Perfect — *action completed before a future moment*

**Rule:** will + have + past participle

- *By June, I will have submitted all my documents.*
- *By the time you arrive, I will have finished the setup.*

---

#### 6. Future Continuous — *action in progress at a specific future moment*

**Rule:** will + be + verb-ing

- *This time next year, I will be studying at a Chinese university.*
- *At 9 AM tomorrow, I will be sitting in the interview.*

---

## Day 4 — Modal Verbs: The Language of Nuance

### Grammar Block (60 min)

**The Core Principle:**
Modal verbs express *attitude toward reality* — not just "what happens" but how certain you are, whether something is allowed, required, or advisable. This is where English becomes subtle and powerful.

---

#### Modal Verbs Overview

| Modal | Core Meanings |
|-------|--------------|
| **can** | ability (present), permission (informal) |
| **could** | ability (past), polite request, possibility |
| **may** | possibility, formal permission |
| **might** | weaker possibility, suggestion |
| **must** | strong obligation (internal), logical deduction |
| **have to** | obligation (external rule/situation) |
| **should** | advice, expectation, mild obligation |
| **ought to** | advice (slightly more formal than should) |
| **will** | strong intention, prediction |
| **would** | conditional, polite requests, past habit |
| **shall** | offers/suggestions (British), legal/formal |
| **need to** | necessity (not quite modal) |
| **dare** | challenge/bravery (rare) |

---

#### Ability: can / could / be able to

- *I can read academic papers in English.* (present ability)
- *I couldn't understand the lecture at first.* (past inability)
- *After six months, I was able to follow every seminar.* (achieved ability in past — use "was able to," not "could," for one specific successful event)
- *I will be able to speak fluently by next year.* (future — "will be able to," not "will can")

---

#### Possibility and Deduction

**Present/Future:**
- *This algorithm might cause a race condition.* (weak possibility, ~30-40%)
- *The server may be overloaded.* (neutral possibility, ~50%)
- *That must be the error — the logs match exactly.* (logical deduction, ~90%)
- *It can't be a hardware problem — we just replaced the components.* (logical impossibility)

**Past:**
- *She might have missed the email.* (possible past event)
- *He must have forgotten to push the changes.* (logical deduction about past)
- *I can't have made that error — I tested every case.* (impossibility in past)

---

#### Obligation and Advice

**Must (internal obligation / strong rule):**
- *You must back up your data before any migration.* (it's critical — speaker's strong opinion or rule)

**Have to (external obligation):**
- *I have to submit the form by Friday.* (external deadline, not my preference)

**Should (advice/expectation):**
- *You should read the Feynman Lectures.* (advice)
- *The results should be ready by tomorrow.* (expectation)

**Shouldn't vs. mustn't:**
- *You shouldn't rush this.* (advice: it's unwise)
- *You mustn't share this data.* (prohibition: it's forbidden)

---

#### Would — Politeness, Hypothesis, Past Habit

- *Would you mind reviewing my code?* (polite request)
- *I would redesign the architecture if I had more time.* (hypothesis — Day 5)
- *We would meet every Monday to sync up.* (past habit — same as Day 2)

## Day 5 — Conditionals: Logic and Hypothesis

### Grammar Block (60 min)

**The Core Principle:**
Conditionals express *cause and effect in time*. Each type encodes a different relationship between reality and possibility. This is the structure behind logical reasoning, analysis, and persuasion — critical for academic writing and speaking.

---

#### Zero Conditional — *universal truth, scientific fact*

**Structure:** If + Present Simple, Present Simple

**When:** The result is always true. No exceptions.

- *If you divide by zero, the program throws an exception.*
- *If temperature drops below 0°C, water freezes.*
- *If the cache is full, the system evicts the oldest entry.*

**Note:** "When" is interchangeable here — *When you divide by zero...* = same meaning.

---

#### First Conditional — *real, likely future scenario*

**Structure:** If + Present Simple, will + infinitive

**When:** A realistic future condition with a probable result.

- *If I pass the language exam, I will apply to Zhejiang University.*
- *If you optimize this query, the response time will improve significantly.*
- *If the weather is good on Saturday, we'll run more laps.*

**Variations with other modals:**
- *If you submit early, you may get an earlier response.* (possibility)
- *If you have time, you should review Chapter 3.* (advice)

---

#### Second Conditional — *unreal/hypothetical present or future*

**Structure:** If + Past Simple, would + infinitive

**When:** Situation is imagined, unlikely, or contrary to present reality.

- *If I had more time, I would study formal verification.* (I don't have time now)
- *If I were a professor at Zhejiang, I would redesign the CS curriculum.* (I'm not)
- *If this algorithm were O(n), it would be faster than anything we have.* (it isn't)

**Important:** Use "were" for all persons in formal/written English: *If I were you...* (not "was")

---

#### Third Conditional — *unreal past, regret or alternate history*

**Structure:** If + Past Perfect, would + have + past participle

**When:** Imagining a different outcome for something that already happened.

- *If I had studied English earlier, I would have applied sooner.*
- *If the team had tested the edge cases, the bug would not have reached production.*
- *If Turing had lived longer, he would have seen his ideas vindicated.*

---

#### Mixed Conditionals — *mixing past and present*

**Type:** If + Past Perfect (past unreal condition), would + infinitive (present result)
- *If I had chosen a different career path, I would probably not be interested in Chinese universities now.*

**Type:** If + Past Simple (unreal present), would + have + pp (past result)
- *If I weren't so focused on engineering, I would have studied philosophy.*

---

#### Unless, Provided, As Long As, In Case

- *Unless you document the code, no one will understand it.* (= If you don't...)
- *I'll apply to Zhejiang, provided I pass IELTS with 6.5+.*
- *The system will scale as long as the database handles concurrent reads.*
- *Back up your work in case the server goes down.* (= because it might)

## Day 6 — Passive Voice + Reported Speech: Academic English

### Grammar Block (60 min)

**The Core Principle:**
These two structures are the backbone of formal, academic, and professional English. If you want to write papers, give presentations, or discuss research, you need both of these fluently.

---

### Part A: Passive Voice

**The Principle:**
Use passive when the *action* or *result* matters more than *who did it*. Academic writing uses passive heavily because it sounds objective and focuses on findings.

**Structure:** to be (in correct tense) + past participle

| Active | Passive |
|--------|---------|
| Researchers *developed* the model. | The model *was developed* by researchers. |
| They *are testing* the system. | The system *is being tested*. |
| Someone *has published* the results. | The results *have been published*. |
| The team *will deploy* the service. | The service *will be deployed*. |
| They *had configured* the server. | The server *had been configured*. |

**When to prefer passive:**
- Unknown agent: *The file was deleted.* (we don't know who)
- Unimportant agent: *The bug was fixed.* (who fixed it doesn't matter)
- Scientific/academic writing: *The hypothesis was tested using...*
- Emphasizing the result: *Three new algorithms were proposed.*

**Passive with modals:**
- *The data must be encrypted before transmission.*
- *This error could have been prevented.*
- *The system should be monitored continuously.*

**Common mistakes:**
- ❌ *The problem was solved by me.* ← grammatically fine, but awkward. Use active: *I solved the problem.*
- ❌ *It was explained me.* → ✅ *It was explained to me.* (indirect object needs "to")

---

### Part B: Reported Speech

**The Principle:**
When you report what someone said, you shift the tense back one step into the past. This is called *backshift*.

**Tense backshift table:**

| Direct Speech | Reported Speech |
|---------------|----------------|
| Present Simple | Past Simple |
| Present Continuous | Past Continuous |
| Present Perfect | Past Perfect |
| Past Simple | Past Perfect |
| will | would |
| can | could |
| may | might |
| must | had to |

**Examples:**
- *"I study algorithms every day."* → He said he *studied* algorithms every day.
- *"I am working on my application."* → She said she *was working* on her application.
- *"I have visited Beijing."* → He told me he *had visited* Beijing.
- *"The exam will start at 9."* → They said the exam *would start* at 9.
- *"You must submit by Friday."* → She said I *had to* submit by Friday.

**Reporting verbs (more precise than "said"):**
- *claim, argue, state, explain, suggest, admit, deny, warn, promise, confirm, announce, insist, recommend*

Examples:
- *The professor explained that passive constructions were more formal.*
- *He argued that AI would replace junior developers within a decade.*
- *She recommended that I apply one month in advance.*

**Questions in reported speech:**
- *"Are you applying to Zhejiang?"* → She asked me *if I was applying* to Zhejiang.
- *"What is your research interest?"* → He asked *what my research interest was.* (no question word order)

## Day 7 — Complex Sentences: The Mark of Fluency

### Grammar Block (60 min)

**The Core Principle:**
Short sentences are clear. Complex sentences are efficient. Fluency in English is largely the ability to connect ideas in a single, structured sentence rather than a series of choppy clauses. This is what distinguishes B1 from C1.

---

### Part A: Relative Clauses

**Defining relative clauses** (no commas) — identify which one

- *The algorithm **that** I wrote runs in O(n log n).*
- *The professor **who** interviewed me teaches machine learning.*
- *The university **where** I want to study is in Hangzhou.*
- *The year **when** I first visited China was transformative.*

**Non-defining relative clauses** (with commas) — add extra information

- *Zhejiang University, **which** is ranked among the top 50 in the world, accepts 200 international students per year.*
- *Turing, **whose** ideas shaped modern computing, was a mathematician.*
- *The conference, **where** I presented my work, attracted 500 researchers.*

**That vs. Which vs. Who:**
- *That* — defining clauses, things and people (AmE)
- *Which* — non-defining clauses, things
- *Who* — people (defining and non-defining)
- *Whose* — possession

**Omitting the relative pronoun (contact clauses):**
When the pronoun is the *object*, it can be dropped:
- *The paper (that) I read yesterday was excellent.* ← "that" can be omitted
- *The professor who gave the lecture...* ← "who" cannot be omitted (it's the subject)

---

### Part B: Participle Clauses

A compact way to combine two related actions or states, typical in academic and formal English.

**Present participle (-ing) = active, simultaneous or causal:**
- *Working on the telemetry system, I learned how critical data integrity is.*
  (= While I was working on the telemetry system, I learned...)
- *Having studied the problem for weeks, I finally found the pattern.*
  (= After I had studied the problem for weeks, I finally found...)

**Past participle (-ed) = passive meaning:**
- *Written in Go, the service handles 100k requests per second.*
  (= Because it is written in Go...)
- *Designed for embedded systems, the library has minimal memory overhead.*

---

### Part C: Infinitive Structures

**Purpose:** *I came to China to understand how innovation scales.*
**Adjective + infinitive:** *This algorithm is difficult to optimize.*
**Too/enough:** *He was too nervous to speak fluently. / He was confident enough to present.*
**It + adjective + infinitive:** *It is essential to understand the fundamentals first.*
**Object + infinitive:** *I want you to review the code. / She asked me to rewrite the section.*

---

### Part D: Subordinating Conjunctions (Complex Sentences)

| Meaning | Conjunctions |
|---------|-------------|
| Time | when, while, as, before, after, until, by the time, once, as soon as |
| Cause | because, since, as, given that |
| Result | so that, such that, therefore |
| Contrast | although, even though, while, whereas, despite, in spite of |
| Condition | if, unless, provided, as long as |
| Purpose | so that, in order to/that |

**Examples (academic style):**
- *Although the model was trained on limited data, the results were surprisingly accurate.*
- *Since I had already worked with embedded systems, adapting to the new hardware was straightforward.*
- *The performance improved significantly once we eliminated the redundant database calls.*

## Notes for Your Live Teacher

Each evening session (45 min) should follow this structure:

**First 5 min:** Collect the student's translation drill on paper or verbally. Correct quickly and move on.

**Next 20 min:** Free conversation on the prepared topics. Do not interrupt every error — correct at natural pause points. Focus on today's grammar structure specifically.

**Next 15 min:** Pick 2-3 sentences the student produced during conversation. Analyze them together: what worked, what to improve, one better version.

**Last 5 min:** Give the student one sentence to produce as a homework test. They write it, you check it.

**Philosophy:** The speaking lesson is not a grammar test. It is a performance. Let the student speak with some errors. Then correct precisely. Fluency is built through *volume of output*, not through fear of mistakes.

## Translation Drill Answer Key Principles

When checking your own translations, ask:
1. Is the tense correct for the intended meaning?
2. Does the sentence sound natural (not word-for-word from Russian)?
3. Is the word order correct? (English: Subject → Verb → Object → Adverbials)
4. Are there unnecessary words? English is more economical than Russian.
5. If using a complex structure — does the clause connect logically to the main sentence?
