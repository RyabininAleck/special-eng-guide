# Physics Vocabulary Lesson 7

## Topic

Pressure, states of matter, and phase changes

## Goal

In this lesson, you learn the vocabulary for describing how matter behaves under different conditions. You already know about thermal energy and heat transfer. Now you learn about pressure, the three states of matter, and how matter changes from one state to another.

## Main Rule

`Pressure` is force applied per unit area.

Basic formula:

`pressure = force / area`

Read it aloud:

`Pressure equals force divided by area.`

Pressure is measured in `pascals` (Pa). One pascal is one newton per square meter.

Matter exists in three main states: `solid`, `liquid`, and `gas`. Each state has different properties because of how particles are arranged and move.

## 1. Pressure

### Brief Explanation

`Pressure` tells us how concentrated a force is. A small force spread over a tiny area creates high pressure. The same force spread over a large area creates low pressure.

For example, a sharp knife cuts easily because the force is concentrated on a very small edge. A flat spatula spreads the same force over a larger area, so it does not cut.

Pressure is important in fluids (liquids and gases). Air pressure pushes on everything around us. Water pressure increases with depth.

### Visual: Pressure = Force / Area

```
   High Pressure              Low Pressure
   ┌─────────┐                ┌─────────┐
   │  Small  │                │  Large  │
   │  Area   │                │  Area   │
   │    ↓    │                │    ↓    │
   │   F/A   │                │   F/A   │
   │   High  │                │   Low   │
   └─────────┘                └─────────┘
```

### Examples

1. Pressure is force per unit area.
2. Pressure is measured in pascals.
3. A sharp knife has high pressure at the edge.
4. Air pressure decreases with altitude.

## 2. States of Matter

### Brief Explanation

`Solid`, `liquid`, and `gas` are the three common states of matter.

`Solid`: particles are packed closely together in fixed positions. Solids have definite shape and volume. Examples: ice, metal, wood.

`Liquid`: particles are close together but can move past each other. Liquids have definite volume but take the shape of their container. Examples: water, oil, mercury.

`Gas`: particles are far apart and move freely. Gases have no definite shape or volume. They fill any container. Examples: air, steam, oxygen.

### Visual: Particle Arrangement

```
   Solid           Liquid          Gas
   ┌─────┐        ┌─────┐        ┌─────┐
   │• • •│        │• • •│        │ •   │
   │• • •│        │ • • │        │   • │
   │• • •│        │• •  │        │ •   │
   └─────┘        └─────┘        └─────┘
   Fixed positions Moving particles Far apart
   Definite shape  Takes container Fills container
```

### Examples

1. A solid has definite shape and volume.
2. A liquid takes the shape of its container.
3. A gas fills the entire container.
4. Water can exist as solid, liquid, or gas.

## 3. Phase Changes

### Brief Explanation

`Phase change` is the transformation of matter from one state to another.

Common phase changes:

- `melting`: solid → liquid (e.g., ice melts into water)
- `freezing`: liquid → solid (e.g., water freezes into ice)
- `evaporation`: liquid → gas (at surface, e.g., puddle dries)
- `condensation`: gas → liquid (e.g., water droplets on cold glass)
- `boiling`: liquid → gas (throughout liquid, e.g., water boils)
- `sublimation`: solid → gas (e.g., dry ice)

Phase changes occur at specific temperatures: `melting point` and `boiling point`.

### Visual: Phase Change Diagram

```
            Melting
   Solid ⇄ Liquid ⇄ Gas
          Freezing

   Melting Point: temperature where solid melts
   Boiling Point: temperature where liquid boils
```

### Examples

1. Melting changes solid to liquid.
2. Freezing changes liquid to solid.
3. Evaporation happens at the surface of a liquid.
4. Boiling happens throughout the liquid.

## 4. Melting and Freezing

### Brief Explanation

`Melting` is the phase change from solid to liquid. When a solid reaches its melting point, its particles gain enough energy to break free from fixed positions.

`Freezing` is the opposite: liquid to solid. When a liquid cools to its freezing point, particles lose energy and arrange into fixed positions.

For water, melting point is `0°C` and freezing point is `0°C`. The temperature stays constant during the phase change.

### Visual: Melting and Freezing

```
   Solid (ice) at 0°C
        │
        │ Heat added
        ▼
   Melting (0°C, constant)
        │
        ▼
   Liquid (water) at 0°C
        │
        │ Heat removed
        ▼
   Freezing (0°C, constant)
        │
        ▼
   Solid (ice) at 0°C
```

### Examples

1. Ice melts at 0°C.
2. Water freezes at 0°C.
3. During melting, temperature stays constant.
4. Freezing is the reverse of melting.

## 5. Evaporation and Boiling

### Brief Explanation

`Evaporation` is the phase change from liquid to gas at the surface. It can happen at any temperature. Faster-moving particles escape from the surface.

`Boiling` is the phase change from liquid to gas throughout the liquid. It happens at a specific temperature: the boiling point. For water at sea level, boiling point is `100°C`.

Evaporation cools things down because the fastest particles leave, lowering the average temperature.

### Visual: Evaporation vs Boiling

```
   Evaporation: Surface only
   ┌─────────────┐
   │ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ ~ │  Gas escapes from surface
   │             │
   │  Liquid     │
   └─────────────┘

   Boiling: Throughout liquid
   ┌─────────────┐
   │ • • • • • • │  Bubbles form inside
   │ • • • • • • │
   │ • • • • • • │
   └─────────────┘
```

### Examples

1. Evaporation happens at any temperature.
2. Boiling happens at a specific temperature.
3. Water boils at 100°C at sea level.
4. Evaporation cools the remaining liquid.

## 6. Latent Heat

### Brief Explanation

`Latent heat` is the heat absorbed or released during a phase change without changing temperature.

During melting, boiling, or other phase changes, the heat energy goes into breaking bonds between particles, not increasing kinetic energy. That is why temperature stays constant until the phase change is complete.

`Latent heat of fusion`: heat needed to melt 1 kg of a substance.
`Latent heat of vaporization`: heat needed to boil 1 kg of a substance.

### Visual: Latent Heat

```
   Temperature vs Heat Added

   Temp │
        │        ___________
        │       /           \
        │      /             \
        │     /               \
        │    /                 \
        │___/                   \___
        └───────────────────────────
             Heat added →

   Flat sections = phase changes (latent heat)
```

### Examples

1. Latent heat is absorbed during melting.
2. Temperature stays constant during phase change.
3. Water has high latent heat of vaporization.
4. Boiling water stays at 100°C until all water boils.

## How The Ideas Connect

- `pressure` depends on force and area.
- matter exists as `solid`, `liquid`, or `gas`.
- `phase changes` transform matter between states.
- `melting` and `freezing` happen at the melting point.
- `evaporation` and `boiling` happen at or below the boiling point.
- `latent heat` is absorbed or released during phase changes.

### Visual: Connection Chain

```
Force + Area → Pressure
                 │
                 ▼
Particle arrangement → State of matter
                 │
                 ▼
Heat added/removed → Phase change
                 │
                 ▼
Latent heat absorbed/released
```

## Common Confusion

### Evaporation vs Boiling

`Evaporation` happens at the surface at any temperature. `Boiling` happens throughout the liquid at a specific temperature.

### Visual: Evaporation vs Boiling

```
   Evaporation: Any temperature, surface only
   Boiling: Specific temperature, throughout liquid
```

Examples:

1. A puddle evaporates slowly at room temperature.
2. Water boils vigorously at 100°C.
3. Evaporation cools the liquid; boiling does not necessarily cool it.

### Melting Point vs Boiling Point

`Melting point`: temperature where solid melts to liquid.
`Boiling point`: temperature where liquid boils to gas.

Examples:

1. Ice melts at 0°C (melting point).
2. Water boils at 100°C (boiling point).
3. Both points are constant for a given pressure.
